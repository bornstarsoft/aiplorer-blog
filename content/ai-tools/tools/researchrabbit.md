## Start With a Paper You Have Read

ResearchRabbit's [seed paper guide](https://learn.researchrabbit.ai/en/articles/18423285-how-to-start-a-search-in-researchrabbit-adding-seeds)
describes growing a search from papers you select. The service
[describes its core recommendations](https://learn.researchrabbit.ai/en/articles/13545485-how-researchrabbit-uses-ai-and-where-we-don-t)
as network-based, not powered by a large language model.

For a first trial, use two public papers on a topic you know. Look for one
earlier reference and one later paper that you did not find with keywords.
Open each original and record why it belongs on the list. If the same authors
or methods dominate your results, change your starting papers and check an
independent search source. Treat the map as a discovery aid, not a complete
literature search.

## Leave Yourself a Starting Point

Save verified references together so you can
[restart from the collection](https://learn.researchrabbit.ai/en/articles/18440884-how-to-start-a-search-from-a-collection)
as the project develops. Keep a separate record of what you searched and when,
especially if the work needs to be reproducible.

The [sharing guide](https://learn.researchrabbit.ai/en/articles/18441080-how-to-share-a-collection-with-a-public-link)
says a public link can show notes and nested collections. Make a separate
reading list with approved public information rather than sharing working
notes or unpublished research. Check the current
[plans](https://www.researchrabbit.ai/pricing) and
[data guidance](https://learn.researchrabbit.ai/en/articles/12796770-data-responsibility-tldr).
Use the [research brief practice path](/use-cases/check-an-ai-research-brief-against-its-sources/)
when turning your reading list into claims that others will rely on.
