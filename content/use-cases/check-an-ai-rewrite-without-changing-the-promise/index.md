## Better Tone, Same Commitment

An easier-to-read message is not necessarily a faithful rewrite. A small
change from "aim to" to "will" can turn a target into a promise. Before
sending an AI-edited message, compare what the two versions commit to,
not just how professional the new one sounds.

This exercise uses invented messages and illustrative rewrites. They are
not outputs measured from a particular service. No real customer data is
needed, and the examples are not legal advice or contract templates.

## Try One Message

**Original**

> We aim to send the draft on 12 November 2026, if the sample files arrive
> by 10 November 2026. This is not a confirmed delivery date. Please keep
> the current review booking until we confirm a replacement.

**A polished but incorrect rewrite**

> We will deliver the draft on 12 November 2026. Please move your review
> booking to that date.

That version removes the dependency, promises delivery and asks the reader
to change a booking. None of those changes is justified by the original.

**One faithful rewrite**

> We are aiming to send the draft on 12 November 2026, provided the sample
> files arrive by 10 November 2026. Delivery is not yet confirmed. Please
> keep your current review booking until we confirm a replacement.

Other wording can be equally valid. The test is whether the date, dependency,
uncertainty and requested action survive together.

### W-01: A Target Is Not A Promise

Keep both dates, the sample-file dependency and the existing booking.
"Subject to availability" is not an adequate replacement for the specific
condition in this message.

### W-02: A Quote Is Not An Approval

Keep the USD 480 quote, what it covers, the excluded item and the instruction
not to start before written approval. A polite acceptance invented by the
rewrite would change the transaction.

### W-03: A Preview Is Not Permission To Publish

Keep the internal-only limit, the named file and the instruction to request
a cleared replacement. A friendlier tone must not broaden permission or
turn an explicit restriction into an optional preference.

## Compare Meaning Before Style

For each actual output, check:

- **Numbers and scope:** Are dates, amounts, file names and included work unchanged?
- **Conditions:** Does each dependency still apply to the same action?
- **Certainty:** Is a proposal still a proposal, rather than an approval or guarantee?
- **Permissions:** Do restrictions and approval requirements remain explicit?
- **Next action:** Is the recipient being asked to do the same thing?

Keep the first output before asking for a correction. Record any missing or
changed clause, then review the repaired version separately. Use **meets
these checks**, **needs correction** or **could not test** for each case;
do not turn three invented messages into a tool score or ranking.

## Return With The Same Reference

Keep version 1 unchanged when rerunning after a relevant tool update or
instruction change. Record the visible settings and exact prompt. If several
things changed at once, do not attribute a different result to just one of them.

For your own workflow, prepare a separate sanitized example and write its
required meaning before testing. A practice pass does not authorize sending
confidential messages to an unapproved service or relying on AI for a binding
agreement. Review important messages yourself and use qualified review where needed.
